AThe Role

What an Enterprise Architect actually does

Orient to the job before the platform mechanics. One sentence frames almost everything that follows: business and IT speak different languages and make decisions independently — an Enterprise Architect exists to force alignment between them.

Enterprise Architect vs. Technical Architect

The two roles get conflated constantly — including on the exam. The distinction that actually matters:

Enterprise ArchitectTechnical Architect
LevelStrategic — aligns IT strategy with business transformation goalsImplementation — the technical details of delivery
ScopeCross-org, cross-cloud, cross-platformOne system, one project, one domain
In tech-heavy orgsCan take on more technical responsibility — the boundary blursDoes not take on EA's strategic or governance responsibilities
Trap

The exam tests this distinction directly: end-to-end IT governance and driving corporate vision belong to EA, never to a technical architect — no matter how senior.

The traits that actually create business value

  • Tech-savvy — advocates for approaches like cloud-native and DevOps to improve efficiency, without chasing technology for its own sake.
  • Data-driven — decisions are based on data and evidence, not intuition or seniority.
  • Agile mindset — balances fast development cycles with the compliance and security the business actually needs.
  • Large scope — makes decentralised and centralised architectures work together, rather than picking one dogmatically.
  • Evangelistic — champions and drives change; resisting change to protect stability is the opposite of this trait.

T-shaped and I-shaped, at the same time

T-shaped — broad enough to connect every business domain and hold a conversation with anyone from finance to infrastructure. I-shaped — deep enough in at least one area to lead a focused project without needing a translator. An EA needs both: broad enough for cross-domain alignment, deep enough to stay credible on at least one thing.

The three C-suite perspectives EA must serve

ExecutiveTheir focusWhat EA delivers for them
CEOEvolving the business, adopting innovationBusiness transformation, M&A support, capability gap closure
CFOReducing complexity, optimising costLandscape rationalisation, TCO reduction, public cloud strategy
CIOManaging complexity, enabling the future stateSimplification, clean architecture, landscape consolidation

Every architecture decision worth defending should be traceable back to at least one of these three. If it isn't, ask why it's being made at all.

Influence without authority

An Enterprise Architect rarely has the authority to make a business unit do anything. The job runs on stakeholder mapping, credibility built through reasoning rather than job title, and artifacts like an Architecture Point of View that make a recommendation stick without a mandate. See 04 Vision & Roadmap for the Stakeholder Map artifact this depends on.

01Current Exam Model

What changed?

Since 2026, SAP has been moving its certification portfolio from multiple-choice recognition tests toward performance-based practical assessments. SAP describes two broad practical formats: System-Based Assessment (execute and validate tasks in a live system) and Scenario-Based Assessment (reason through a connected business situation). P_SAPEA uses the Scenario-Based Assessment format — typically completed either through an AI-guided roleplay interview or by recording a video for human review.

Important

Do not hard-code exam mechanics. Question counts, timing, and pass marks for the legacy multiple-choice version of this exam (40 questions, 3 hours, 80% pass mark, Wanderlust case study) do not reliably describe the current Scenario-Based Assessment. The exact structure, duration, attempt limits and scoring method are shown on the official P_SAPEA certification page and the Scenario-Based Assessment FAQ. Check both immediately before your exam.

02Decision Method

KNOW → RECOGNIZE → DECIDE → DEFEND

Use the same mental loop for every topic.

❓ What is it?

Learn the concept, artifact, method or architecture layer.

🔍 When does it matter?

Spot the signal in a scenario: duplication, risk, strategic change, integration complexity, regulatory constraint, and so on.

⚡ What would you do?

Select an approach that fits the business objective, constraints, maturity and target state.

💡 Why this choice?

Explain rationale, alternatives, trade-offs, risks, sequencing and validation.

Example

"Given [objective] and [constraints], I recommend [decision] because [reason]. The main trade-off is [trade-off]. I would manage the risk by [mitigation] and validate it through [evidence]."

04Skill Area 2

Architecture Vision & Roadmap

The architect's job is not simply to draw a target state. The job is to create a credible path from the current situation to a better business outcome.

Vision

  • Business objectives
  • Stakeholders
  • Current-state pain
  • Target capabilities
  • Scope and constraints
  • Success measures

Roadmap

  • Prioritised initiatives
  • Dependencies
  • Sequencing
  • Value and risk
  • Transition states
  • Governance and decision points
Exam Tip

When the scenario gives you many problems, do not solve them all at once. Establish the strategic objective, identify dependencies, then sequence the work.

05Skill Area 3

Business Architecture

Start with what the business needs to be able to do before jumping to software.

Definition

Capability

What the business is able to do, independent of the detailed process used to do it.

⚠️ Trap: confusing a capability with a process or application.

Definition

Process

How work flows from one activity to another to create an outcome.

⚠️ Trap: optimising a process without checking whether it supports the strategic objective.

When reading a case, ask:

  • What business outcome is changing?
  • Which capabilities must improve or appear?
  • Which processes are creating the problem?
  • Which stakeholders own the outcome?
  • What evidence would prove the architecture is working?

06Skill Area 4

Data, Application & Technology Architecture

📊 What information?

Ownership, quality, lifecycle, privacy, residency, integration and access.

⚙️ What capabilities are supported?

Application responsibilities, boundaries, interfaces, duplication and fit with business capabilities.

🖥️ What enables it?

Platforms, infrastructure, security, connectivity, operations and non-functional requirements.

Definition

Clean Core

Use the core product as intended and avoid unnecessary modifications that make upgrades and lifecycle management harder. Where appropriate, extensions can be designed outside or alongside the core using SAP's supported extensibility approaches.

Definition

Public vs private cloud

Do not memorise "public is good" or "private is good." Evaluate standardisation, fit-to-standard expectations, required flexibility, operational model, integration, lifecycle and business constraints.

Integration & regulatory thinking

  • Identify the business event or data exchange first.
  • Then decide the integration responsibility and pattern.
  • Check security, privacy, residency and regulatory constraints.
  • Prefer the simplest sustainable architecture that meets the requirement.

07Connected Case

The Wanderlust-style case

Use this fictional case to practise the same reasoning pattern without relying on memorised questions. It is inspired by SAP's classic case-study format, not a reproduction of any current exam content.

Scenario

A global manufacturer wants to enter a fast-growing electric-vehicle market. Its business units operate independently, several ERP systems have evolved separately, customer data is duplicated, and leadership wants faster product launches without losing local regulatory control.

The CIO asks for an architecture recommendation and a realistic transformation roadmap.

Six linked decisions

  • Objective: What is the primary business outcome?
  • Constraints: Which business, technology, regulatory and organisational constraints matter most?
  • Business: Which capabilities and processes must change?
  • Architecture: What target-state principles and boundaries would you propose?
  • Roadmap: What should happen first, and why?
  • Governance: What decisions, stakeholders and measures keep the transformation on track?

Push your reasoning further

Before you write anything down, be ready to defend your call against these — this is the kind of pointed follow-up a review board or an AI-guided roleplay will actually ask:

  • The CIO wants one global ERP for speed; regional leaders want to keep local systems for regulatory control. Which one gives, and what do you tell the side that loses?
  • Customer data is duplicated across the separate ERPs today — does unifying it require a single system, or can duplication be resolved a different way?
  • "Faster product launches" and "no loss of local regulatory control" can pull in opposite directions. Where exactly is that tension, and what's your first sequencing move to relieve it?
  • Six months after you present this roadmap, what's the one metric that would tell the CIO it's working — and what would tell them to stop and reconsider?
Important

📌 Do the case before reading examples. Your first objective is not to find a perfect architecture. It is to demonstrate coherent reasoning.

15Case Study

Wanderlust GmbH case hub

SAP's own reference case study for the P_SAPEA exam, organised into the three things you actually need to hold in your head at once: the situation, who the stakeholders are, and where the architecture is heading.

Important

📌 Wanderlust GmbH is a real, publicly-published SAP case study — not a fictional company invented for this workspace. Read the source directly at learning.sap.com/enterprise-architect before the exam. It is unconfirmed whether the current 2026 Scenario-Based Assessment still references Wanderlust by name — see 01 Exam Model & Format. Treat this hub as a structured summary to study from, not a substitute for reading the original.

Wanderlust GmbH is a German-headquartered manufacturer of conventional fuel-driven cars, with manufacturing facilities and sales operations globally. It's renowned for best-in-class engineering — less so for aftermarket customer service — and has had limited success so far expanding into electric vehicles.

  • Expanding into EVs via the "Smart Battery" initiative — value proposition, cost structure, revenue streams, partners and channels are all still being worked out with stakeholders.
  • Legacy landscape: three separate SAP ECC instances, one per continent, evolved independently.
  • The EA practice is at a "nascent" maturity level — no dedicated team, no formalised governance yet.
  • CEO — focused on evolving the business into new business models, particularly the EV/Smart Battery expansion.
  • CIO — goals centre on Lead-to-Cash capabilities: marketing analytics, lead management and campaign management, especially for the seldom-used legacy marketing application that can't carry the online marketing transformation forward.
  • Top priority: stakeholder involvement. With EA at nascent maturity and no existing stakeholder map, identifying who the key stakeholders and owners are comes before any tooling or technical decision — see Discover the Enterprise Architect Role.
  • Transition path: legacy ECC → SAP S/4HANA Cloud for the digital core, with new capability built on SAP BTP — see the Clean Core Decision Engine for which extension type fits which requirement.
  • SAP Signavio maps the business processes (including the Lead-to-Cash variants relevant to Wanderlust's B2C and made-to-order models).
  • SAP LeanIX documents the IT application portfolio, giving the Business-IT alignment a nascent EA practice needs before it can make credible investment recommendations.

Six connected decisions run through the case. Each one constrains the next — this is the "unit of study is the decision" principle applied to a single continuous scenario.

  1. EA maturity → Stakeholder Map first. Nascent maturity, no stakeholder map, no Architecture Board yet. An architecture without identified owners is a drawing, not a plan — see Discover the Enterprise Architect Role.
  2. Process-to-capability sequence. Business Process Modeling (BPM) and the Business Capability Model (BCM) are linked by assigning both to shared Enterprise Domains, then ensuring BPM business activities are enabled by BCM business capabilities — never the reverse.
  3. Lead-to-Cash capability alignment. The CIO's online-marketing transformation maps to three named capabilities: Marketing Campaign Management, Lead Management, and Marketing Analytics — capabilities first, SaaS product selection (e.g. a marketing cloud tool) second.
  4. Organisation map → Business Roles & Personas. The CEO wants internal teams and the dealer network activated for the marketing transformation. A generic org chart is too high-level; extending it into specific business roles and personas gives clear ownership boundaries and drives change adoption.
  5. Smart Battery alignment via Business Model Canvas. Value proposition, cost structure, revenue streams, partners and channels were developed in isolation. A Business Model Canvas pulls all nine dimensions into one page so they can be reconciled together.
  6. Scoping the IBP quick win. SAP Integrated Business Planning is identified as a fast win for demand/supply planning. Before implementation: a Fit-Gap Assessment (what can't be met out-of-the-box), a defined Solution Context, and an Aspirational Solution Concept — all three baseline the Statement of Architecture Work.

Practice defending out loud, not just recognising the right answer on paper — that's what the current scenario format actually rewards.

Stakeholder says

"We're expanding our EV dealer portal. Buy the CRM license today so developers can start building — why waste time mapping 'business capabilities' first?"

Your defense

Buying the license first lets the product dictate the business strategy — a Principle 1 violation. Mapping capabilities first defines what Wanderlust must do to support EV dealers; only then can we select the CRM module and size what it needs to integrate with. Skip the sequence and you get redundant applications and expensive rework later.

Developer says

"I need logic close to the S/4HANA core. A direct database trigger is faster than a side-by-side BTP extension, and I've documented my code — why block the change?"

Your defense

Documentation doesn't change the outcome: a direct table trigger breaks the two-way Clean Core guarantee, and the next automated cloud release will break your trigger or the release itself. If the logic genuinely needs to run close to the core, we build it as Developer Extensibility via RAP against released, upgrade-stable APIs — see the Clean Core Decision Engine.

Project manager says

"We're running agile sprints. The TOGAF ADM is a slow, sequential waterfall — it doesn't fit our timeline."

Your defense

The ADM is iterative by design — within phases, between phases, and between full cycles, not a single pass. We tailor the depth per area: a competitive differentiator like Smart Battery gets a full deep-dive, while standard supporting processes run at a lighter tailoring level. That gives technical discipline without sacrificing sprint speed.

08Defense Pattern

The 9-step architecture defense

1. Context

"The situation I understand is…"

2. Objective

"The outcome we need is…"

3. Constraints

"The constraints that shape the decision are…"

4. Recommendation

"I would recommend…"

5. Architecture rationale

"This fits because…"

6. Trade-off

"The main trade-off is…"

7. Risk

"The largest risk is…"

8. Roadmap

"I would sequence the work as…"

9. Validation

"I would validate the decision through…"

Tip

If you get stuck, return to: business objective → constraints → decision → rationale → trade-off → next step.

Since the current exam is a spoken/recorded roleplay, practise saying this pattern out loud against the clock — not just writing it down.

🎯 Scenario-Based Assessment Drill

Three questions in the format the current exam actually uses — a scenario, a choice, and an immediate explanation of why the SAP-prescribed answer wins over a plausible-sounding generic-cloud-architect distractor.

Question 1 of 3
An architecture guideline demands a clean-core strategy for future extensions to Wanderlust's digital core. S/4HANA upgrades must not break extensions, and extensions must not break upgrades. How is this achieved?
✅ Correct answer: B
Why: That two-way guarantee is the whole point of clean core: the digital core stays upgrade-stable and the extensions survive releases without rescue projects. Direct table access is an upgrade-breaker, and forbidding extensions entirely isn't a real architecture — it's giving up.
Question 2 of 3
Wanderlust's CIO asks where to begin architecting the online marketing transformation whose scope is still unclear. Which business architecture step does SAP's methodology put first?
✅ Correct answer: C
Why: Under SAP's principle of mapping business and IT separately, capabilities describe what the business must be able to do, and processes describe how they do it. Product selection and infrastructure sizing are both downstream decisions — they can't come first because nothing has been scoped yet.
Question 3 of 3
As Wanderlust's Chief Enterprise Architect, you are running an EA practice at "nascent" maturity with no existing stakeholder map. What is identified as your topmost priority?
✅ Correct answer: B
Why: At nascent maturity, an architecture without identified owners and key stakeholders is just an artifact, not a plan. Stakeholder mapping must precede tooling and development — see the Wanderlust Case Hub for exactly this scenario played out in full.

Case 1: Electric Vehicle Manufacturer Transformation

Architect Level Complex

📌 Scenario

A traditional auto manufacturer is pivoting to electric vehicles. They have:

  • Legacy ERP for internal combustion engine production
  • New battery tech R&D that needs agile development
  • Direct-to-consumer sales model (no dealers)
  • Software-defined vehicle features (OTA updates)
  • Global regulatory compliance requirements
  • Existing dealer network pushing back

Push your reasoning further

  • The existing dealer network is losing revenue under direct-to-consumer sales. Whose concern do you address first — the dealers demanding protection, or the CEO demanding speed to market?
  • OTA software updates ship on a completely different cadence than a physical vehicle. Does that push you toward two separate architectures, or one platform serving both cadences?
  • R&D needs agile development; manufacturing needs stability. Where exactly do you draw the boundary between them?

Your Task

Using the 9-step defense pattern, provide a complete architecture recommendation:

  1. Context — Describe the situation
  2. Objective — Define the business outcome
  3. Constraints — List key constraints
  4. Recommendation — Your architectural approach
  5. Architecture rationale — Why it fits
  6. Trade-off — What you're sacrificing
  7. Risk — Biggest risk and mitigation
  8. Roadmap — Sequence of work
  9. Validation — How to measure success
📖 View Model Answer

1. Context: Traditional manufacturer transitioning to EV with legacy systems and new business models.

2. Objective: Launch EV product in 24 months with direct-to-consumer sales and OTA capabilities.

3. Constraints: Existing dealer agreements, regulatory approvals, R&D timeline, budget constraints.

4. Recommendation: Two-speed IT - maintain legacy for core manufacturing, build new cloud-native platform for EV and D2C.

5. Rationale: Allows innovation without destabilizing existing business.

6. Trade-off: Higher short-term investment for long-term flexibility.

7. Risk: Cultural resistance from legacy IT teams.

8. Roadmap: Phase 1: Stand up cloud platform; Phase 2: EV customer portal; Phase 3: Integration with manufacturing; Phase 4: Full transition.

9. Validation: Time-to-market for new features, customer satisfaction scores, cost per vehicle.

  • Strong regulatory compliance culture

  • High security requirements

  • Starting with read-only APIs is the safe move. What's the actual trigger condition for when you're allowed to open write access?
  • Security compliance is non-negotiable in banking. Where does that constraint stop you from doing something the business explicitly asked for?
